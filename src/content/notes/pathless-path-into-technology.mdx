The first technical thing I ever did professionally was teach.

Not in a classroom. In a campus lab – the New Media Center – where student workers ran workshops on software and programming for anyone who showed up and wanted to learn. The NMC was tucked into a building most students walked past without going in. It had rows of workstations, a small equipment lending operation, and a certain quality of quiet that felt different from a library. People came in with specific problems – they needed to edit a video, learn to use a camera, figure out why their InDesign file was breaking. The job was to help them get unstuck.

I was there because someone thought I knew enough about Illustrator and Photoshop to be useful, and because the schedule fit around fine arts with minors in English and Psychology, which felt less like a decision and more like what was already true about me. I loved drawing. I loved painting. I loved the particular problem of sculpture – that you had to commit to material before you fully knew the form. I could have gone further in any of those directions. Then a visiting artist started a series of classes under an emphasis called "new genres," and something clicked that I hadn't been looking for.

New genres was an odd category. It had to be. The list of what counted as valid work was long and a little absurd in the best possible way: conceptual art alongside Arduino prototyping, 3D modeling alongside video editing, physical computing alongside whatever you wanted to make with a Raspberry Pi. There was no coherent framework for what belonged in the room. That was, I eventually understood, the point. For a kid who had grown up online – who felt equally at home tinkering on a computer as sketching in a notebook, who had spent more time than he'd admit modding games and building things in browser windows – it was the first academic environment that didn't ask him to pick a lane and commit to it. I didn't know that at the time. I just knew I didn't want to leave.

Meanwhile, the NMC work was doing something to me that I wasn't tracking consciously. Teaching Illustrator. Teaching Photoshop. Walking someone through why the pen tool works the way it does, why Bezier handles move the way they move, why the software was designed around this particular set of assumptions about how a person would want to create. You can't hide behind fuzzy comprehension when someone is watching you explain something and waiting for it to make sense. The act of making a thing legible to another person is its own education. I was learning the tools more deeply by teaching them than I had by using them.

Then a grad student started pitching work to the NMC. He had ideas – real ones, product-shaped ones, with actual user problems underneath them – and he needed people who could build toward them. I don't remember exactly how I got folded in. Probably I was nearby and willing. What we assembled was not a team in any formal sense. It was a collection of people with wildly different capabilities: CS students who could write a backend, business students who could frame a problem in terms someone would pay to solve, people like me who arrived from somewhere adjacent and sideways. We built a question-and-answer campus social network. A time-logging kiosk that read student ID cards. A tool for migrating LMS data from Blackboard to Canvas when the university switched platforms.

None of us had done any of this before. That was, it turned out, part of the point.

There's a version of this story where I describe what I contributed as "design thinking" or "the creative perspective" and leave it there. That version is accurate but incomplete. What I actually brought was an instinct for experience – for what a thing should feel like to encounter for the first time, what it communicated before a word was read, whether the interaction asked something unreasonable of the person on the other side. At the time I thought of that instinct as something I was smuggling into the room. A soft skill, if I was being generous with myself. A liability, if I was being honest about how the world seemed to value things.

What I understand now is that it was load-bearing. The team worked because it was genuinely diverse – not in a performance of diversity, but in actual capability and point of view. The CS students knew how to build. The business students knew how to frame problems in terms someone would pay to solve. I knew how to ask what the thing should feel like before anyone knew what it was going to do. On a team building something from nothing, that question might be the first one worth asking.

I didn't fully appreciate any of this until much later. In the moment, I was just trying to keep up.

After graduation, the NMC network followed me out the door. Immediately people were asking me to design and build apps, prototypes, pitch decks. Someone offered me a job at a local dev shop. I said yes without fully knowing what the job was, which turned out to be the right posture – because what it was, was a place where I would have to learn HTML, CSS, and JavaScript, and I didn't yet know enough about those things to have an informed opinion about how hard they were. That ignorance was useful. I just started and kept going. It lasted nearly two years.

Then someone offered me a six-month contract and I quit. The project was a health and human services app – a system that replaced paper-based tracking with software, for an organization that had been doing things the old way for a long time. It was the first time I was building software in any real sense, not just shipping websites. There were actual users with actual workflows we were disrupting, actual stakes if the thing didn't work. It mattered in a way that felt new.

When the contract ended, there was nothing. No job to return to, no next thing already lined up. You find the next thing or you don't work. That pressure clarified a lot about what I was actually capable of, and a lot about what kind of work I actually wanted to do.

The years that followed were a mix of contracts and full-time roles. Some of the bigger shops in town turned me down – I remember some of those rejections specifically, remember how they landed. Others reached out. I spent time on the UX team at Omaha Steaks, helping build a functional CSS framework and learning what it means to ship code in an ecommerce environment, where every decision touches revenue in some direction. The feedback loop is fast and unambiguous. You know quickly whether you were right. Eventually the same companies that had passed on my post-graduation applications began approaching me. I worked with Adidas, Red Bull, Teva, Sanuk, Hollister, Outdoor Research. Each engagement a new environment, a new set of constraints, a new version of the question I had first learned to ask in that campus lab: what should this thing feel like?

Most recently I've been at Marriott International, deep inside Platform Engineering, building and supporting an internal developer platform that gives thousands of engineers self-service access to infrastructure. It is the largest and most complex environment I have ever worked in – the scale of it took time to really feel, the way the scale of something very large only becomes real once you've tried to change a small part of it and traced how far the effects reach. It happened to be where I was standing when the AI development revolution arrived, which has sent me down a path that now includes prompt engineering, generative UI, and agentic workflow architectures. Each one feeling, in its own way, like walking into a new genres class for the first time. The same mix of not-quite-knowing what counts, and not wanting to leave.

That's roughly how it happened. No plan. No clear pivot point where one chapter ended and another began. Just a series of environments that kept asking more than I expected, and a willingness – or maybe an inability to resist – to say yes anyway.

The career I ended up with is not one I could have designed in advance. That used to feel like a liability. I've come to think it's the opposite.
